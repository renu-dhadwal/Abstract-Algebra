1  Preliminaries

The integers are among the most familiar objects in mathematics. We learn to calculate with them long before we ask why their arithmetic has the form that it does. Familiarity, however, can conceal structure. In this chapter, we step back from calculation and examine the principles on which arithmetic in () rests.

Our purpose is not to review elementary arithmetic. Instead, we will ask questions such as:

  • Why can every integer be divided by a positive integer to produce a unique quotient and remainder?
  • Why can a greatest common divisor always be expressed as an integer linear combination?
  • Why is factorization into primes unique?
  • What does it mean to regard two integers as the same modulo (n)?

The starting point is the well-ordering principle: every nonempty set of positive integers has a least element. This apparently simple property has powerful consequences. It gives a method for proving existence, underlies mathematical induction, and leads to a proof of the Division Algorithm. The Division Algorithm, in turn, produces the Euclidean algorithm, Bézout’s identity, and much of the elementary theory of divisibility.

Congruence modulo (n) introduces a further change of perspective. Rather than studying integers individually, we group together integers that have the same remainder upon division by (n). The resulting congruence classes form the set (/n), where addition and multiplication can be performed directly on classes. This will provide our first substantial example of a quotient construction.

Thus, the integers serve two purposes. They supply concrete examples against which later definitions can be tested, and they illustrate the process of abstraction itself: familiar operations and patterns are isolated, expressed as general principles, and then transported to new mathematical settings.

## The Integers

The integers provide a natural starting point for abstract algebra. Their
arithmetic is familiar, but many facts that we routinely use—division with
remainder, the Euclidean algorithm, and unique factorization—depend on
structural properties that deserve to be stated and proved carefully.

We write

$$
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
$$

for the set of positive integers and

$$
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}=\{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}
$$

for the set of nonnegative integers.

### The Well-Ordering Principle

> **Well-Ordering Principle.**
>
> Every nonempty subset of $\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ has a least element.

Thus, if $S$ is a nonempty set of positive integers, there is some $m\in S$
such that

$$
m\leq s
$$

for every $s\in S$.

For example, consider

$$
S=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}:n^2\geq 30\}.
$$

The set $S$ is nonempty, and its least element is $6$.

Both hypotheses in the Well-Ordering Principle matter. The set must be
nonempty, and its elements must be positive integers. The set $\mathbb{Z}$,
for example, is nonempty but has no least element.

The following equivalent version will be convenient.

> **Well-Ordering Principle for nonnegative integers.**
>
> Every nonempty subset of $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ has a least element.

Indeed, if such a set contains $0$, then $0$ is its least element. If it does
not contain $0$, it is a nonempty subset of $\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$, so the original
Well-Ordering Principle applies.

The importance of the Well-Ordering Principle is not that it helps us find the
least element of a set that has already been explicitly listed. Its real
importance is as a proof method. A typical argument has the following form:

1. Construct a nonempty set of nonnegative or positive integers satisfying a
   desired condition.
2. Use the Well-Ordering Principle to select its least element.
3. Show that if this element did not have some additional property, then a
   still smaller element of the same set could be constructed.
4. Conclude that the least element must have the desired property.

The proof of the Division Algorithm will be our first substantial use of this
method.

### Divisibility

We begin with the relation of divisibility.

> **Definition.**
>
> Let $a,b\in\mathbb{Z}$. We say that $b$ **divides** $a$, and write
>
> $$
> b\mid a,
> $$
>
> if there exists $k\in\mathbb{Z}$ such that
>
> $$
> a=bk.
> $$
>
> If no such integer exists, we write $b\nmid a$.

When $b\mid a$, we also say that $b$ is a **divisor** or **factor** of $a$,
and that $a$ is a **multiple** of $b$.

For example,

$$
3\mid 12
$$

because $12=3\cdot4$, while

$$
5\nmid 12
$$

because there is no integer $k$ for which $12=5k$.

Negative integers may also be divisors. For example,

$$
-3\mid 12
$$

because $12=(-3)(-4)$.

Some special cases deserve attention. For every $b\in\mathbb{Z}$,

$$
b\mid 0,
$$

because $0=b\cdot0$. On the other hand,

$$
0\mid a
$$

only when $a=0$. Thus, under our definition, $0\mid0$, but $0\nmid a$
whenever $a\neq0$.

The following elementary properties will be used repeatedly.

> **Proposition.**
>
> Let $a,b,c,m,n\in\mathbb{Z}$.
>
> 1. $a\mid a$.
> 2. If $a\mid b$ and $b\mid c$, then $a\mid c$.
> 3. If $a\mid b$ and $a\mid c$, then
>    $$
>    a\mid(mb+nc).
>    $$
> 4. If $a\mid b$ and $b\mid a$, then
>    $$
>    a=\pm b.
>    $$

**Proof.**

1. Since $a=a\cdot1$, we have $a\mid a$.

2. Suppose $a\mid b$ and $b\mid c$. Then there exist $r,s\in\mathbb{Z}$
   such that
   $$
   b=ar
   \qquad\text{and}\qquad
   c=bs.
   $$
   Therefore,
   $$
   c=(ar)s=a(rs).
   $$
   Since $rs\in\mathbb{Z}$, it follows that $a\mid c$.

3. Suppose $a\mid b$ and $a\mid c$. Write
   $$
   b=ar
   \qquad\text{and}\qquad
   c=as
   $$
   for some $r,s\in\mathbb{Z}$. Then
   $$
   mb+nc=m(ar)+n(as)=a(mr+ns).
   $$
   Since $mr+ns\in\mathbb{Z}$, we conclude that
   $$
   a\mid(mb+nc).
   $$

4. Suppose $a\mid b$ and $b\mid a$. If either $a$ or $b$ is zero, then
   both are zero, and the conclusion follows. Otherwise, there exist
   $r,s\in\mathbb{Z}$ such that
   $$
   b=ar
   \qquad\text{and}\qquad
   a=bs.
   $$
   Substitution gives
   $$
   a=a(rs).
   $$
   Since $a\neq0$, we obtain $rs=1$. The only integers whose product is
   $1$ are $1,1$ and $-1,-1$. Hence $r=\pm1$, and therefore $b=\pm a$.

$\square$

The integers $1$ and $-1$ are the only integers that divide every integer.
They are called the **units** of $\mathbb{Z}$.

Two integers are called **associates** if one is obtained from the other by
multiplication by a unit. Thus, in $\mathbb{Z}$, the associates of $a$ are
$a$ and $-a$. The final part of the preceding proposition says that two
integers divide one another precisely when they are associates.

### The Division Algorithm

Ordinary arithmetic suggests that an integer can be divided by a positive
integer to produce a quotient and a remainder. The next theorem states that
such a quotient and remainder always exist and are unique.

Despite its traditional name, the Division Algorithm is a theorem as well as
a computational procedure.

> **Division Algorithm.**
>
> Let $a\in\mathbb{Z}$ and let $b\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$. Then there exist
> unique integers $q$ and $r$ such that
>
> $$
> a=bq+r
> $$
>
> and
>
> $$
> 0\leq r<b.
> $$
>
> The integer $q$ is called the **quotient**, and $r$ is called the
> **remainder**.

**Proof.**

We first prove existence. Consider the set

$$
S=\{a-bk:k\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ and }a-bk\geq0\}.
$$

Thus, $S$ consists of all nonnegative integers that can be written in the
form $a-bk$.

The set $S$ is nonempty. If $a\geq0$, then taking $k=0$ gives $a\in S$. If
$a<0$, take $k=-|a|$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
a-bk
  &=a+b|a|\\
  &=-|a|+b|a|\\
  &=(b-1)|a|\\
  &\geq0,
\end{aligned}
$$

because $b\geq1$. Hence $a-bk\in S$.

By the Well-Ordering Principle for nonnegative integers, $S$ has a least
element. Denote this least element by $r$. Since $r\in S$, there exists
$q\in\mathbb{Z}$ such that

$$
r=a-bq.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
a=bq+r.
$$

By the definition of $S$, we already know that $r\geq0$. It remains to show
that $r<b$.

Suppose, to the contrary, that $r\geq b$. Then

$$
r-b\geq0.
$$

Moreover,

$$
r-b=(a-bq)-b=a-b(q+1).
$$

Therefore, $r-b\in S$. But $r-b<r$, contradicting the fact that $r$ is the
least element of $S$. Consequently,

$$
0\leq r<b.
$$

This proves existence.

We now prove uniqueness. Suppose that

$$
a=bq+r
$$

and also

$$
a=bq'+r',
$$

where

$$
0\leq r<b
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
0\leq r'<b.
$$

Subtracting the two expressions for $a$, we obtain

$$
b(q-q')=r'-r.
$$

Since $0\leq r,r'<b$, we have

$$
|r'-r|<b.
$$

If $q-q'\neq0$, then

$$
|b(q-q')|\geq b,
$$

which is impossible because $b(q-q')=r'-r$. Hence $q-q'=0$, so $q=q'$.
It then follows that $r=r'$.

Therefore, the quotient and remainder are unique.

$\square$

For example,

$$
17=5\cdot3+2,
$$

so division of $17$ by $5$ gives quotient $3$ and remainder $2$.

When the dividend is negative, the condition $0\leq r<b$ remains in force.
For example,

$$
-17=5(-4)+3.
$$

Thus, the quotient is $-4$ and the remainder is $3$. Although

$$
-17=5(-3)-2
$$

is a valid equality, it is not the quotient–remainder representation required
by the Division Algorithm because the proposed remainder $-2$ is negative.

If division by an arbitrary nonzero integer $b$ is desired, the theorem may
be written in the form

$$
a=bq+r,
\qquad
0\leq r<|b|.
$$

Requiring $b>0$ avoids carrying the absolute value throughout the statement
and proof.
